# Chapter 3: Automating the Repetitive

## Find What Is Eating Your Time

Before you automate anything, you need to know what is worth automating. Track your tasks for one week and tag everything that falls into these categories:

- **Content creation** — writing social posts, newsletters, blog articles, emails
- **Research** — market analysis, competitor checks, prospect hunting, trend tracking
- **Reporting** — metrics dashboards, weekly summaries, client updates
- **Communication** — responding to emails, scheduling, follow-ups
- **Data entry** — moving information between tools, updating spreadsheets, filing records

If you are honest with yourself, at least 60% of your working hours go to tasks in these categories. That is not high-leverage work. That is maintenance.

## The Automation Hierarchy

Not everything should be fully autonomous on day one. Think of automation as a ladder with four rungs:

**Level 1: Manual** — You do the task yourself, from scratch, every time. This is where most founders live.

**Level 2: Templated** — You create templates, checklists, and standard processes so the task takes half the time. Better, but still manual.

**Level 3: Scheduled** — An automation runs on a schedule and delivers results to you. You review, adjust, and approve. This is the sweet spot for most tasks.

**Level 4: Fully Autonomous** — An agent handles the entire workflow end to end, including decisions and execution. Reserve this for low-risk, high-frequency tasks.

Your goal is to move every recurring task up at least one level. Most tasks should land at Level 3 within your first month.

## Real Examples That Work

### Daily SEO Check (Level 3)

A scheduled task runs every morning at 7am. It checks your website's search rankings for target keywords, flags any significant changes, and sends you a summary via Telegram or email. You glance at it over coffee. If something needs attention, you handle it. If not, you move on.

**Time saved:** 20-30 minutes per day of manual checking.

### Weekly Content Calendar (Level 3)

Every Monday morning, an automation generates a content calendar for the week. It analyzes what performed well last week, identifies trending topics in your niche, and drafts a 7-day plan with post ideas, hooks, and suggested formats. You review it, make adjustments, and execute.

**Time saved:** 2-3 hours per week of planning.

### Email Inbox Monitor (Level 3)

An automation scans your inbox on a schedule and categorizes messages by priority. High-priority messages from clients or prospects get flagged immediately. Low-priority newsletters and notifications get summarized. Spam and irrelevant messages get tagged for deletion.

**Time saved:** 30-45 minutes per day of inbox management.

### Weekly Competitor Analysis (Level 3)

Every Friday, an automation checks your top 3-5 competitors for changes: new blog posts, pricing updates, product launches, social media activity, and job postings (which signal what they are investing in). You get a one-page summary of what changed and what it means for your business.

**Time saved:** 1-2 hours per week of manual research.

## Setting Up Scheduled Tasks

The simplest way to run automations on a schedule is with cron jobs. If you are using an agent framework, most of them support scheduled execution natively. If you are using Make or Zapier, they have built-in scheduling for every workflow.

The pattern is always the same:

1. **Define the trigger** — A schedule (daily at 7am, every Monday, etc.)
2. **Define the task** — What the automation should do (fetch data, analyze it, generate a report)
3. **Define the output** — Where the results go (Telegram message, email, dashboard update)
4. **Define the fallback** — What happens if something fails (retry, notify you, skip)

Start with daily and weekly schedules. Do not try to automate things that happen irregularly or require significant judgment. Those are for agents (Chapter 4).

## The Golden Rule of Automation

Automate the tasks you do every single day first. Then the ones you do every week. Then monthly.

The biggest mistake founders make is trying to automate complex, occasional tasks while still doing simple, daily tasks by hand. If you spend 30 minutes every morning doing something an automation could handle in 30 seconds, that is over 180 hours per year. Fix that first.

Your daily routine should be: wake up, check the reports your automations delivered overnight, make decisions, and focus on the work that actually moves the needle. Everything else should be handled before you open your laptop.
